## CONSTITUTIONAL LAW – I

### UNIT I — Meaning and Concept of Constitution and Constitutionalism

1. Constitution — Meaning

A Constitution is the fundamental and supreme law of a country which establishes:

- Structure of government
- Powers and functions of governmental organs
- Relationship between different organs
- Distribution of powers between Union and States
- Rights and freedoms of individuals
- Limitations on governmental power

The Indian Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949 and came into force on 26 January 1950.

Functions of a Constitution

1. Establishes government
   - Creates Parliament, Executive and Judiciary.
2. Distributes powers
   - Especially between Union and State governments.
3. Limits governmental power
   - Fundamental Rights restrict arbitrary State action.
4. Protects individual liberty
   - Articles 14–32 provide constitutional protection.
5. Establishes rule of law
   - Government itself is subject to law.
6. Provides mechanisms for constitutional change
   - Article 368 provides amendment procedure.

2. Constitutionalism

Constitutionalism means the principle that governmental power must be limited and exercised according to constitutional rules.

A country may have a Constitution but still lack genuine constitutionalism.

Constitution vs Constitutionalism

Constitution

- Fundamental legal document
- Creates government
- Contains powers
- Can exist formally
- Example: Indian Constitution

Constitutionalism

- Political/legal philosophy
- Limits government
- Controls exercise of powers
- Requires respect for constitutional limitations
- Example: Rule of law, separation of powers, judicial review

Elements of Constitutionalism

- Rule of law
- Limited government
- Separation of powers
- Judicial review
- Fundamental Rights
- Independent judiciary
- Democratic government
- Accountability
- Constitutional supremacy

3. Classification of Constitutions

A. Written and Unwritten

Written Constitution

- Rules are substantially contained in a written document.
- Example: India, USA.

Unwritten Constitution

- Constitutional rules arise from statutes, conventions, judicial decisions and customs.
- Example: United Kingdom.

India has a written and detailed Constitution.

B. Rigid and Flexible

Rigid Constitution

- Difficult amendment procedure.
- Example: USA.

Flexible Constitution

- Can be amended comparatively easily.
- Traditional example: UK.

The Indian Constitution is partly rigid and partly flexible.

For example:

- Some provisions can be amended by simple majority.
- Some require special majority.
- Some require special majority + ratification by at least half of the States.

C. Federal and Unitary

Federal Constitution

- Powers constitutionally divided between Union and States.
- Written Constitution
- Supremacy of Constitution
- Independent judiciary
- Usually bicameral legislature

Unitary Constitution

- Central government possesses predominant authority.

India has a federal structure with strong unitary features.

4. Sources and Framing of the Indian Constitution

Major Sources

The Constitution borrowed several features from other constitutional systems.

Country — Feature borrowed

- UK — Parliamentary government, Rule of Law, Cabinet system
- USA — Fundamental Rights, Judicial Review, Independence of Judiciary
- Ireland — Directive Principles of State Policy
- Canada — Federation with strong Centre, Residuary powers
- Australia — Concurrent List, Freedom of trade and commerce
- Germany — Emergency provisions
- USSR/Russia — Fundamental Duties
- South Africa — Amendment procedure
- Japan — Procedure established by law

Government of India Act, 1935

It was one of the most important sources.

Features influenced by it included:

- Federal structure
- Office of Governor
- Public Service Commissions
- Emergency provisions
- Administrative framework
- Distribution of legislative powers

5. Constituent Assembly

The Constitution was framed by the Constituent Assembly.

Important personalities

- Dr. B.R. Ambedkar — Chairman, Drafting Committee
- Dr. Rajendra Prasad — President of Constituent Assembly
- Jawaharlal Nehru — introduced Objectives Resolution
- Sardar Patel — important role in integration and constitutional arrangements
- B.N. Rau — Constitutional Adviser

Drafting Committee

Chairman: Dr. B.R. Ambedkar

The Constitution was finally adopted on: 26 November 1949

It came into force on: 26 January 1950.

6. Preamble

The Preamble describes the basic philosophy and objectives of the Constitution.

It begins:

"We, the People of India…"

This establishes the principle of popular sovereignty.

Important words

India is described as:

- Sovereign
- Socialist
- Secular
- Democratic
- Republic

The objectives are:

Justice

- Social
- Economic
- Political

Liberty

- Thought
- Expression
- Belief
- Faith
- Worship

Equality

- Status
- Opportunity

Fraternity

- Dignity of individual
- Unity and integrity of nation

42nd Constitutional Amendment, 1976

Added:

- Socialist
- Secular
- Integrity

Is Preamble part of Constitution?

Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala (1973)

The Supreme Court held that:

- Preamble is part of the Constitution.
- It can be used to interpret constitutional provisions.
- It is subject to the basic structure doctrine.

Berubari Union Reference (1960)

Initially, the Supreme Court had taken the view that the Preamble was not a source of substantive power.

This position was subsequently changed in Kesavananda Bharati.

7. Salient Features of Indian Constitution

1. Written Constitution — India has one of the world's most detailed written constitutions.

2. Length and Detail — It contains extensive provisions dealing with government, rights, federalism, elections, emergency, judiciary, local government, services.

3. Sovereignty of Constitution — All governmental organs derive their authority from the Constitution.

4. Parliamentary Government — The executive is responsible to the legislature.

5. Federal Structure — Powers are divided between Union and States.

6. Strong Centre — The Union has considerable powers, particularly during emergencies.

7. Fundamental Rights — Part III guarantees enforceable fundamental rights.

8. Directive Principles — Part IV contains socio-economic objectives for governance.

9. Fundamental Duties — Article 51A contains duties of citizens.

10. Independent Judiciary — The Supreme Court and High Courts exercise judicial review.

11. Judicial Review — Courts can invalidate unconstitutional laws and executive actions.

12. Universal Adult Franchise — Citizens meeting the constitutional voting requirements participate in elections.

13. Single Citizenship — India generally provides for single citizenship.

14. Secular State — The State does not establish an official religion and guarantees religious freedom.

8. Is Indian Constitution Federal?

India is generally described as: "Federal in structure but with strong unitary features."

Federal features

- Written Constitution
- Supremacy of Constitution
- Division of powers
- Independent judiciary
- Bicameral Parliament
- Constitutional supremacy
- Rigid amendment procedure for certain provisions

Unitary features

- Strong Union
- Single Constitution
- Single citizenship
- Integrated judiciary
- Governors appointed by Union
- Parliament's power regarding States in certain circumstances
- Emergency provisions
- Residuary legislative power with Union

Important Case

S.R. Bommai v Union of India (1994) — The Supreme Court recognised federalism as an important/basic feature of the Constitution.

Exam conclusion

India cannot be classified as purely federal or purely unitary. It is better described as a federal system with a strong centralising tendency.

## UNIT II — CONSTITUTIONAL ORGANS OF GOVERNMENT

A. PARLIAMENT

1. Composition of Parliament

Article 79 provides that Parliament consists of:

1. President
2. Rajya Sabha
3. Lok Sabha

Lok Sabha (House of the People)

- Members are directly elected by citizens.
- Direct election
- Five-year normal term
- Can be dissolved
- Money Bills originate only in Lok Sabha
- Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to Lok Sabha

Rajya Sabha (Council of States)

- Permanent House
- Not subject to dissolution
- One-third members retire every two years
- Represents States and Union Territories
- Vice-President is ex officio Chairman

2. Parliamentary Sovereignty

The Indian Parliament is not absolutely sovereign like the traditional British Parliament. It is subject to Constitution, Fundamental Rights, judicial review, federal limitations, basic structure doctrine. Constitutional supremacy > Parliamentary supremacy.

3. Parliamentary Privileges

Article 105 deals with privileges of Parliament; Article 194 deals with State Legislatures. Important privileges include freedom of speech in Parliament, immunity for certain parliamentary proceedings, right to regulate internal proceedings, power to punish for contempt/breach of privilege.

Important cases: M.S.M. Sharma v Sri Krishna Sinha; Raja Ram Pal v Speaker, Lok Sabha (2007).

B. EXECUTIVE

President of India (Articles 52–62)

- Constitutional/formal head of the Union Executive.
- Article 53 vests executive power of the Union in the President.
- Under Article 74, the President ordinarily acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers.

Powers of President

1. Executive powers: appointment of PM, ministers, governors, AG, CAG, Election Commissioners, etc.
2. Legislative powers: summoning/proroguing Parliament, dissolving Lok Sabha, assent to Bills, ordinance power (Art.123).
3. Financial powers: Money Bill recommendation, budget presented in President's name.
4. Judicial powers: Article 72 grants pardon, reprieve, remission, commutation.

Governor (Articles 153–162)

- Constitutional head of a State.
- Powers: executive, legislative, financial, judicial/pardoning, ordinance-making under Article 213.
- Governor has limited discretion in certain situations.

C. JUDICIARY

- Integrated system: Supreme Court → High Courts → Subordinate Courts.

Supreme Court (Article 124)

Jurisdictions: original (Art.131), writ (Art.32), appellate, special leave (Art.136), advisory (Art.143), review (Art.137).

High Courts (Article 214)

- Article 226: High Courts can issue writs for fundamental and other legal rights (broader than Art.32).

Independence of Judiciary: tenure, removal procedure, salaries charged on Consolidated Fund, separation from executive, judicial review, contempt powers, institutional autonomy.

Appointment of Judges: Collegium system (Three Judges Cases: S.P. Gupta (1981), Second Judges Case (1993), Third Judges Case (1998)); NJAC struck down in 2015 (Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v Union of India).

## UNIT III — DISTRIBUTION OF POWERS BETWEEN UNION AND STATES

- Legislative powers divided through Union List, State List, Concurrent List (Seventh Schedule).
- Article 246 distributes subjects between Parliament and State Legislatures.
- Residuary powers: Article 248 gives Parliament power over matters not enumerated in State or Concurrent Lists (residuary to Union).

Parliament's power over State subjects: Articles 249–253, 356 (President's Rule).

Administrative relations: Articles 256–263; Inter-State Council (Art.263) for coordination.

Financial relations: Articles 268–293; Finance Commission (Art.280) recommends distribution of tax revenues and grants-in-aid.

Important constitutional doctrines: Territorial Nexus; Pith and Substance; Colourable Legislation; Repugnancy; Harmonious Construction.

## UNIT IV — EMERGENCY, AMENDMENTS AND FREEDOM OF TRADE

A. Freedom of Trade, Commerce and Intercourse (Articles 301–307)

- Article 301: trade, commerce and intercourse throughout India shall be free; subject to constitutional restrictions (Arts.302–304).
- Important cases: Atiabari Tea Co. v State of Assam; Automobile Transport (Rajasthan) Ltd. v State of Rajasthan; Jindal Stainless Ltd. v State of Haryana (2016).

B. Emergency Provisions (Part XVIII)

Types: National Emergency (Art.352), President's Rule (Art.356), Financial Emergency (Art.360).

National Emergency (Art.352): proclaimed for war, external aggression, or armed rebellion (44th Amendment removed "internal disturbance"). Effects include expanded Union powers, legislative authority over State subjects; certain Fundamental Rights suspension (Art.358 & 359) with protections for Arts.20 and 21.

President's Rule (Art.356): applied where constitutional machinery of a State has failed; subject to judicial review (S.R. Bommai v Union of India).

Financial Emergency (Art.360): never proclaimed so far.

C. Amendment of Constitution (Art.368)

Types: simple majority amendments; special majority; special majority + state ratification.

Doctrine of Basic Structure: Kesavananda Bharati (1973) — Parliament cannot destroy the Constitution's basic structure.

Important cases: Golaknath (1967), Kesavananda (1973), Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain (1975), Minerva Mills (1980), I.R. Coelho (2007).

## CONSTITUTIONAL LAW – II

### UNIT I — FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS – I

- Fundamental Rights in Part III (Arts.12–35); enforceable by courts.
- Evolution: colonial experience, freedom movement, Nehru Report, Constituent Assembly debates.

Article 12: Definition of State — includes Government of India, Parliament, State Governments, State Legislatures, local authorities, and other authorities under control of Government of India.

Instrumentality/Agency Doctrine: courts expanded Art.12 to cover bodies functioning as instruments/agencies of government (e.g., R.D. Shetty v International Airport Authority; Ajay Hasia v Khalid Mujib).

Article 13: constitutional supremacy over inconsistent laws — foundation for judicial review. Doctrines: severability, eclipse.

### UNIT I — ARTICLE 14

- Equality before law (negative) and equal protection (positive).
- Reasonable classification: intelligible differentia and rational nexus.

Modern Article 14 doctrine prohibits arbitrariness (E.P. Royappa); Maneka Gandhi linked Arts.14,19,21.

Compensatory discrimination permitted (Article 15(3),(4),(5), Article 16(4),(4A)).

### ARTICLE 19 — FREEDOMS

- Six freedoms: speech & expression; peaceful assembly; association; movement; residence; profession.
- Freedom of speech (Art.19(1)(a)) includes press, political expression, artistic and online expression; subject to reasonable restrictions (Art.19(2)).
- Important cases: Romesh Thappar; Bennett Coleman; Shreya Singhal.

### UNIT II — FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS II

ARTICLE 21

- Right to life and personal liberty (requirement: procedure established by law — must be fair, just, reasonable).
- Maneka Gandhi expanded Art.21; many rights read into Art.21 (privacy, livelihood, shelter, clean environment, legal aid, speedy trial, dignity, protection against custodial violence).
- Important cases: Francis Coralie Mullin; Olga Tellis; D.K. Basu; K.S. Puttaswamy.

Article 21A — Right to Education (86th Amendment); RTE Act, 2009; cases: Mohini Jain; Unni Krishnan.

Article 22 — safeguards on arrest & detention; preventive detention allowed with safeguards.

ARTICLE 23 — Trafficking and Forced Labour — prohibition; key cases: PUDR; Bandhua Mukti Morcha.

ARTICLE 24 — Child Labour — prohibition of employment of children below specified age in factories, mines, hazardous employment.

ARTICLE 25 — Freedom of Religion — freedom of conscience, practice, propagation subject to public order, morality, health, and other FRs.

Essential Religious Practices Doctrine (Shirur Mutt).

Articles 26–28: religious denominations, tax exemptions, religious instructions in educational institutions.

Articles 29–30 — Minority Rights (language, culture, educational institutions) — key cases: St. Stephen's; T.M.A. Pai; P.A. Inamdar.

### UNIT III — RIGHT TO CONSTITUTIONAL REMEDIES

- Article 32: Dr. B.R. Ambedkar called it the "heart and soul" of the Constitution.
- Article 226: High Courts can issue writs for fundamental and other legal rights (broader than Art.32).

Five constitutional writs: Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Certiorari, Prohibition, Quo Warranto — purposes explained.

Public Interest Litigation (PIL): allows litigation for public interest; important cases: S.P. Gupta; Bandhua Mukti Morcha; PUDR.

### UNIT IV — DIRECTIVE PRINCIPLES AND FUNDAMENTAL DUTIES

Directive Principles (Arts.36–51, Part IV): not enforceable by courts but fundamental in governance (Art.37).

Classification: Socialist principles (Arts.38,39,39A,41,42,43); Gandhian principles (Arts.40,43,46,47,48); Liberal-Intellectual (Arts.44,45,48A,49,50,51).

Fundamental Rights vs DPSP: Champakam Dorairajan held FRs prevail in conflict; later jurisprudence balanced both (Kesavananda, Minerva Mills).

FUNDAMENTAL DUTIES (Art.51A): introduced by 42nd Amendment; includes respect for Constitution, national symbols, protect environment, develop scientific temper, etc. Courts have used duties for interpretation and strengthening protections (e.g., M.C. Mehta on environment).
